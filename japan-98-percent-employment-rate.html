Japan's 98% Graduate Employment Rate Isn't a Miracle. It's Demographic Arithmetic.

October 29, 2026 · Daniel Zou · Japan series №1 · ~9 min read

I fly to Japan more than almost any other market I cover in APAC recruiting. And over the past two years, hiring in Tokyo, I've felt something shift that I can no longer ignore: HR managers at small and mid-sized companies have started begging students to sign.

The numbers back the feeling. According to the joint survey by Japan's Ministry of Education (MEXT) and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the employment rate for university students graduating in spring 2025 hit 98.0% — a second straight year above 98%, and a first since the survey began in 1997.

Online, plenty of people use this figure to argue that Japan has finally escaped its "lost three decades," that the economy is back. In China, some middle-class parents even cite it as a reason to send their kids to study in Japan — "copy Japan's homework."

Let me take this number apart for you. The conclusion first: 98% is not an economic miracle. It is a demographic arithmetic problem. And it's a problem China will have to hand in its own answer to, roughly fifteen years from now.

First, look at how the 98% is actually calculated

The denominator of this "employment rate" is not all graduates. It's only the graduates who say they want to work — in 2025, that was 75.6% of the class. The 98% measures how many of those students landed jobs.

The structure matters even more. Inside the same 98%, two opposite worlds coexist. At large companies with over 5,000 employees, the jobs-to-applicants ratio is 0.34 — three candidates fighting for one opening. The competition is as brutal as ever. At small and mid-sized companies with fewer than 300 employees, the ratio is 6.50 — seven openings chasing a single student.

So Japan's real picture is not "the economy is so good that everyone has a job." It's that there are too few young people to go around. I call this "denominator prosperity": the numerator (jobs) has barely moved, while the denominator (young workers) shrinks every year. The rate drifts toward a perfect score on its own.

This is not an economics story. It's a population story.

Three buckets of cold water on the viral version

Chinese-language commentary on this topic gets at least three things wrong. Some cold water is in order.

Bucket one: the inheritance tax is not "a flat 40%." Japan's inheritance tax runs on eight progressive brackets, from 10% to 55%. Only the portion of an estate above 600 million yen (≈$4M) touches the 55% bracket, and there is a sizable exemption first — 30 million yen (≈$200K), plus 6 million yen per heir. Most ordinary families' estates never enter the high brackets at all. "Grandpa dies, the government takes 40%" confuses the top marginal rate with the typical rate.

Bucket two: "every Japanese elder takes 2 million RMB to the grave" is an average illusion. The real figures from the Statistics Bureau's household survey: median savings for households headed by someone in their sixties are about 6.5 million yen — roughly 300,000 RMB (≈$43K). For households in their seventies, about 8 million yen. The mean is indeed over 20 million yen, but that figure is dragged upward by wealthy families at the top. The median is what most Japanese elders actually have in their pocket. The viral line about "35 million yen carried into the coffin" comes from a cautionary projection by management writer Kenichi Ohmae. It was a warning, not a statistic.

Bucket three: crediting the recovery to "wealth redistribution as the old die off" is too theatrical. The mainstream consensus on why Japan finally broke out of deflation these past two years involves three things. Shunto — the annual spring wage-offensive in which unions and big employers negotiate pay — delivered the largest raises in over three decades, restarting the wage-price cycle. The Tokyo Stock Exchange pressed listed companies to reform their governance. And a weak yen set off an inbound tourism and consumption boom. Demographic change is the background music here, not the main melody.

But three things in that judgment are right

Cold water poured. Now let me fish out the parts that hold up.

First, Japan really is China's reference case, walking ten to twenty years ahead. That judgment stands. Looking at Japan is looking at a future that parts of China are already arriving at.

Second, the transmission chain of the "low-desire society" is real. The elderly clutch their savings and don't spend — money stops circulating — industries contract — young people get no chances — they lie flat, skip marriage, skip children — the next generation of young people shrinks further. Japan ran this entire loop once, end to end, and every link has data behind it. Ohmae's books — The Low-Desire Society and Silver Economics — are exactly about this. Worth reading.

Third, the silver economy is a genuinely large, high-certainty opportunity — and a more concrete one than the viral posts describe. That is the real subject of this piece. Let's get to it.

One map of Asia: young, middle-aged, old

Zoom out, and you see a striking time map.

Vietnam's median age is about 32. It is a young man. China's is about 40 — middle-aged. Japan's is about 49 — old.

Three countries, like the same person at three different ages. I wrote recently about Vietnam — a young country where people's eyes shine but factories can't find line leaders. Japan is the opposite extreme: mature industries, complete institutions, but the waiters, taxi drivers, and convenience-store clerks you see on the street are, in large numbers, silver-haired seniors.

In Vietnam, you see China's yesterday. In Japan, you see China's day after tomorrow. China's today sits right in the middle: 320 million people over 60, 23% of the population — already fully into moderate aging.

So the question is not "should we face the silver economy." It's "where do you plan to stand inside it."

The real opportunity list: the gap, the new job titles, and the pay

I'm a lifer in recruiting. I only offer judgments that have sources.

The gap. Over the next five years, China faces a shortfall of more than 5 million eldercare workers. Within the existing workforce, 83% are aged 40 to 59; workers under 30 make up less than 2%. An industry that is severely short-staffed — and that almost no young person wants to enter — is exactly the kind of industry that policy and capital will flood with resources.

The new job titles. China's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security has, several years running, designated eldercare-related roles as officially recognized new occupations: elder-ability assessors, eldercare service specialists, bathing assistants for the elderly, dementia care workers. In 2026 it added the "aging-friendly renovation designer" — someone who retrofits homes so old people can live in them safely. When new job titles cluster in one field, the labor market's signal light is turning green.

The pay. Platform data shows that roles like eldercare service specialist and renovation designer typically pay 7,000 to 12,000 RMB a month (≈$970–$1,650); with project-management experience, that can reach 15,000 (≈$2,080). Not high — yet. But follow Japan's trajectory: the premium on professional-service roles climbs steadily as the industry matures.

The total market. China's silver economy already runs above 7 trillion RMB (≈$1 trillion), and official projections have it passing 30 trillion RMB (≈$4.2 trillion) by 2035. Sales revenue for senior travel services grew 26% year over year in the first half of last year.

One caveat. What this industry lacks most is not caregivers. It's "the middle layer that understands old people" — station managers who can lead a care team, assessors who can design a service plan, product managers who can translate an elder's needs into a product. The same thing I said about Vietnam: wherever an industry rises, the first shortage is always shop-floor supervisors.

Practical advice for two kinds of readers

For families choosing a direction for their kids: use Japan as a reference, but don't copy its homework. When Japan entered deep aging, its per-capita GDP was around $40,000. China's today is a little over $10,000 — getting old before getting rich. That means China's silver-economy opportunity is not in high-end eldercare. It's in "mass-affordable plus technology": using technology to push the price of professional services down. Whoever pulls that off wins the next decade.

For seasoned workplace veterans: the silver economy is one of the few tracks where age doesn't depreciate — it appreciates. Understanding life, carrying real experience, being able to empathize with an 80-year-old: these are precisely the things young people cannot fake. Your second spring doesn't live only in AI. It's on this boat, too.

One honest feeling to close. Every time I fly back from Japan, I feel like I've been looking into a brightly polished mirror. What it reflects is not "what we will become." It's "what we still have time to do."

Japan's today is an exam paper China hands in fifteen years from now. The only difference is that we've already seen their answer sheet.

Next in this series: the country's other door. While the front door fights over fresh graduates, the side door is quietly ushering out wave after wave of workers over 45. Tokyo's white-haired taxi drivers walked out through that side door.

Do you know any young people going into eldercare? I'd like to hear about them.
